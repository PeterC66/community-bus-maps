A working system, looking for someone to run it as a Community Interest Company.

Co-founder opportunity — transit mapping CIC

We have built a system that produces clear, accurate bus maps for a town or a single place, for print and for the web, at a small fraction of what a design agency charges — and, more to the point, keeps them right as services change. That combination is what is missing: most small towns have no single sheet showing every operator's buses, and the few that do have one that is out of date.

We are looking for an entrepreneurial individual or a small team to take it on and run it as a standalone Community Interest Company.

What comes with it: the whole working system, openly licensed; a documented way of producing and maintaining maps; and a clear social mission in public-transport access and digital inclusion. What does not come with it is a set of instructions about how to run it — the business model, the funding, and who to sell to would be yours to shape.

Interested in closing the transport-information gap? info@busmaps.uk.

What the asset actually is

Stated concretely, because this is the part that decides whether the above is an opportunity or a wish. Everything here exists and runs today.

🗺️

A working engine

Seven towns and five places have been built with it, from a small market town to High Wycombe, with an eighth town drafted and going through verification before it joins them. It is deterministic: the same data always produces the same sheet, byte for byte, with no network and no AI at drawing time — which is what makes a print file reproducible a year later.

🚦

A gate that prices the job first

Every area is scored before the expensive work starts — how many lines, how tightly they bunch, how much of the network would end up hidden — and returns green, amber or red, with a documented ladder of remedies for a red one. Very few small suppliers can tell a customer how hard their town is before quoting.

🔁

A portal, not a folder of files

Self-serve editing inside a safe subset, three approval gates (organisation, map request, publication review) and a monthly maintenance cycle where the customer sees the change, previews it and accepts it. The recurring half is the product; the one-off sheet is what everyone else sells.

📖

Documented to be handed over

An operations handbook and six runbooks — create a map, onboard a customer, review and publish, run the monthly cycle, handle messages, handle an incident — plus a developer guide and a deploy runbook that includes the restore procedure step by step, which is the part usually missing.

🔓

Open source, publicly

The portal is on GitHub under Apache-2.0, with its history. That is deliberate: an asset a successor can read, fork and audit is worth more than one they have to be given, and it makes the CIC's independence real rather than contractual.

📶

Built on open data

Bus services from the UK Bus Open Data Service (Open Government Licence); streets and landmarks from OpenStreetMap (ODbL, attributed on every sheet). No licensed dataset to re-negotiate, and no supplier who can switch the input off.

Why a CIC, and not a startup

The mission comes first, and it has to stay first

The organisations that most need a bus map — parish councils, community transport, small market towns — are the ones least able to pay for one. The organisations that can pay comfortably are science parks, hospital campuses and business districts. A CIC lets the second group cross-subsidise the first as a matter of governance rather than goodwill, and the asset lock means that cannot quietly be reversed later.

It also makes the selling easier

Council procurement has to weigh social value, and a CIC makes that part of the evaluation easy to answer: the social objective is a matter of registration rather than a claim in a tender document. It also opens grant and foundation funding that a sole trader simply cannot apply for. The legal form is not a compromise here; it is the better commercial fit.

Who would suit it

Background

Sustainable transport, GIS and mapping, or civic tech. You do not need to have written the software — but you do need to be able to read it, or to have someone who can.

Shape

An individual or a small team. A retired or transitioning transport planner is close to an ideal fit: the hard part is not the code, it is knowing which door to knock on inside a local authority.

The honest bit

This is a one-person project today. Succession is exactly the problem being solved — a map that is only maintained while one person keeps maintaining it is not really maintained. Knowing that going in matters more than any skill on this list.

What is not resolved

Listed here rather than raised on a second call, because a serious candidate will find them anyway and both of us would have wasted the time.

One data source needs its terms settled

The official feeds are openly licensed and fine. One supplementary source — the excellent bustimes.org, used to cross-check route geometry — has terms that have not been formally cleared for commercial use. Nothing is charged for today, so nothing turns on it yet; it would have to be closed before an invoice is raised, either by agreement, by payment, or by doing without it.

The entity itself is undecided

This page describes what a CIC could be handed. There is no CIC yet, and no decision has been taken between incorporating one and running the same thing as a small commercial supplier. That is genuinely open — and it means an incoming founder would be shaping the answer rather than inheriting it.

There is a third thing worth saying plainly: there are no customers yet. The system is a pilot and every organisation and map on this site is our own, labelled as such. What is proven is that the thing works and can be run; what is not proven is that anyone will pay for it.
