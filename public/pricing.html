Pricing

What this would cost, if and when it costs anything.

It is free during the pilot

Nobody has been charged and no price has been offered. Every organisation that joins the pilot does so at no cost, and you would never be billed by surprise: if charging is ever introduced it would be with notice, and you would keep the sheets you had already downloaded. What the pilot means.

Everything below this box is written in the future tense on purpose. It describes how a price would be worked out — so that you can judge whether it would ever be affordable — not what anyone is paying.

What you would be buying

Three separable things. Most suppliers sell only the first.

🖨️

Print-ready sheets

A4 at 300 dpi, plus the SVG, for each output you have turned on — reviewed by a person before you can download them. A one-off deliverable: useful the day it lands.

🔁

Keeping them right

The monthly cycle: the data is re-read, changes to your routes are shown to you, you preview and accept, and your colours and labels carry over. This is the part that stops a map on a noticeboard quietly becoming wrong.

🌐

A public page

Each reviewed map gets a page anyone can view, print and link to, carrying your organisation's name and a form so readers can tell you if something looks off.

The maintenance is the product. A beautiful map that was accurate two years ago is worse than no map, because people trust it. The sheets are what gets you started; the monthly cycle is what the money would actually be for.

How a price would be arrived at

Two parts, and no figure quoted before we have looked at your town.

One-off

A build fee

Charged once, when your map is first made. It would be quoted after we survey the area and score how hard it is to draw — never before.

Recurring

An annual fee

Covering the monthly update cycle, the review on each new edition, and the public page. Per map, per year — not per sheet and not per print run.

Why the survey comes first

Towns differ enormously. Before any of the expensive work starts, the system scores the area on how many services run, how tightly they bunch in the centre, and how much of the network ends up hidden underneath other lines. The score comes out green, amber or red, and a red town genuinely costs several times what a green one does.

That score is what a quote would be based on. It is the difference between a price we can show you the reasoning for and a number someone guessed — and it is why we would rather survey first and quote second. See what a red town looks like, and what we do about it.

Would it be good value?

Against a design agency

An agency's cost is a cartographer's time on your map, once. That produces a fine map and a standing bill: every time a route moves, someone has to be paid to redraw it. The drawing here is generated from the data, so the intention is to price an order of magnitude below that and to have the updates covered rather than quoted each time.

The honest version

For most market towns the alternative is not a cheaper supplier. It is nothing — no single sheet showing every operator's buses on one map exists, and residents assemble the picture themselves from two or three websites. That makes this an easy thing to compare favourably against, and a hard thing to find a budget line for. Which is why the next section exists.

Who normally pays for this

The commonest objection is "we have no budget for maps" — and it is usually wrong. The money tends to sit in a budget nobody thinks of as a mapping budget.

Councils & transport authorities

Bus Service Improvement Plan funding carries a passenger-information commitment that has to be reported on. Parish and district work is often reachable through S106 or CIL money attached to a development, or straight from the precept.

Hospitals, campuses & schools

Green travel plans and sustainability budgets, and the travel-plan obligations attached to planning permission — which sometimes require public-transport information to be published.

Business & science parks

Tenant service charges and amenity budgets. This sits alongside the cycle racks and the shuttle bus as part of what tenants are already paying for.

Town centres, BIDs & attractions

BID levy budgets earmarked for access and the visitor experience; for attractions, the visitor-marketing budget, since the map is what gets someone there without a car.

Bus operators & community transport

The marketing budget — offset directly against what an external designer would have been paid for the same artwork.

Not sure which is you?

Tell us who you are and what you need and we will say plainly whether we think there is a route to funding it. Ask us.

We do not print

You get the print-ready file; you use your own printer, or a local print shop, or the copier in the office. That is deliberate. Printing through us would add a markup to something you can buy better and cheaper locally, and it would tie the price of a map to the price of paper.

You can reprint at will — it costs you nothing to run off more copies whenever you need them. Keep the sheet as issued, credits included: the map is built from licensed data (OpenStreetMap, BODS) that requires attribution, so please don't alter it or remove the acknowledgements. Full terms of use.
