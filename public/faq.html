Frequently asked questions

Can’t see your question? Ask us — we’re happy to help.

What does “pilot” mean here?

It means the system is built and working end to end, but it has never been used in anger. Specifically, and plainly:

  • We have no customers. No organisation has signed up. Every map on this site was produced by us, to build and test the system.
  • The example maps are ours. They are real output — nothing is mocked up — but no council or business publishes them, and they are not maintained as live travel information. Anything shown as Sample is one of these.
  • There is no service level. No uptime promise, no response times, no guarantee the monthly refresh happens on time. It is run by one person alongside other work.
  • Things may change or stop. We may alter how it works, pause it, or withdraw it. We would give reasonable notice and you would keep the sheets you had already downloaded.

What is not provisional: the maps themselves are built from official open data and reviewed by a person before publication. The pilot label is about the service around them, not about the care taken over them.

When we have organisations using it and a track record to point at, this notice and the banner at the top of every page come off.

What is BusMaps.uk?

A system that produces clear, printable bus maps for a place — a whole town or parish (an area map), or a single point such as a shop, school or centre (a place map) — and is designed to help you keep them current as services change. It is currently a pilot.

Who can apply?

Any organisation whose community uses the bus: councils, shops, businesses, schools, event organisers, charities, attractions and more. Every application is reviewed and approved before an account is set up. During the pilot we are looking for a small number of organisations willing to try it and tell us what is wrong with it.

How much does it cost?

Nothing. It is free during the pilot. We may introduce charges in future, but always with notice — you would never be billed by surprise. Our pricing page explains what you would be buying and how a price would be worked out, if and when there is one.

Why would the price depend on my town?

Because towns differ enormously in how hard they are to draw. Before the expensive work starts, the system scores the area — how many services run, how tightly they bunch in the centre, how much of the network ends up hidden under other lines — and returns green, amber or red. A red town takes several times the work of a green one, so a quote would be based on that score rather than on a guess. You can see a red town and what we do about it on the examples page.

How many maps can I have?

To start with, each organisation can have one area and a few places. Each map is approved individually. We would expect to raise these limits once the pilot has run its course.

What can I change myself?

You can choose which of the standard outputs you want, recolour and relabel routes, turn landmarks on or off, and accept the monthly updates — then download print-ready files. The more delicate layout work (straightening lines, nudging labels) we do for you, so the result reads properly in print.

What are the four outputs?

An internal geographic map (drawn to the streets), an internal schematic (straightened and tidied), an internal diagram (tube-map style), and an external map showing where the buses go. You pick which ones you want for each map — except the diagram, which is hand-finished and asked for rather than switched on.

Why does the tube-map diagram cost extra?

Because it is the one output that is not simply generated. The geographic and schematic maps and the external map are produced deterministically from the data: the same data always draws the same sheet, with nobody's hand on it.

The diagram is different. The software solves the network's topology — which line meets which, and where — but a readable tube map then needs junctions moved by eye until the whole thing sits well on the page. Those hand placements are stored as pins, and they are ours to maintain: when a route is extended or a service withdrawn, the topology changes and the pins have to be re-judged against it.

So the diagram would never be a one-off drawing charge. It is drawing time in the first build and drawing time in every update — which is why, when charging starts, it would be quoted separately rather than folded into the price of the map. Everything is free during the pilot; what is true today is that it is offered in the editor with an Ask us button rather than a tick-box, so nobody switches it on and only afterwards finds out what it involves.

How up to date are the maps?

The intention is a refresh from official data every month, and that is how the system is built to work — though during the pilot the cadence is not guaranteed. When something changes on your routes, you’ll see “a change to review”, preview it, and accept it — your colours and labels carry over automatically.

Where does the data come from?

Bus routes, stops and timetables come from the UK Bus Open Data Service (BODS), published under the Open Government Licence. Streets, rivers and landmarks come from OpenStreetMap (© OpenStreetMap contributors, ODbL). Live times should always be checked with the operator or at bustimes.org.

Do you use AI?

Not to make decisions about your map. The maps themselves are deterministic: the geographic, schematic and external outputs are drawn straight from the BODS and OpenStreetMap data by ordinary code, with no AI involved and no network call made at the moment a sheet is generated — the same data always produces the same sheet. The tube-map diagram is hand-placed by a person, not by AI. And every map, whichever outputs it uses, is checked by a person before it can be published.

Where AI has been involved is in building the software itself — it was written with the help of an AI coding assistant, the same as a growing share of software generally is. That is a statement about how the code was written, not about how your map gets drawn.

Does our map get a public web page?

Yes. Once a version is reviewed, it gets a page on our public site — the sheets to view or download, your organisation’s name, badge, colour and website, and a form so readers can tell you if something looks wrong. It also appears in our published maps gallery. You control your public details, and you can take a map off the public site with one tick without affecting your own copies.

Can I print them?

Yes — every map is produced as a print-ready A4 sheet at 300 dpi (and as an SVG), sized and coloured for real printing.

Do you guarantee they’re correct?

No. A person reviews every map before it can be printed — a check that it looks right, not an independent verification against timetables — and we build only from official open data. Routes and times still change, and this is a pilot with no warranty behind it. These maps are a guide: please encourage readers to confirm live times before travelling.

What do you do with my details?

We use them only to consider your application and to set up and support your maps. We don’t sell them or use them for anything else, and you can ask us to delete them.
