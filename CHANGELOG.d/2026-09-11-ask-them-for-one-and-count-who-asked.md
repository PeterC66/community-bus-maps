---
date: 2026-09-11
title: "\"Ask them for one\" — a letter the reader sends, and a count of the places nobody has a map of"
---

The directory panel added earlier today tells a reader who else publishes a bus map for their area. For roughly half the authorities in it the honest answer is **nobody does**, and until now that is where the page stopped. Two things follow it: the letter that asks, and the count of how often anybody had to.

- **A ready-to-send letter, on the row that said nobody publishes one.** It is addressed to the authority the directory just named, it says what that authority publishes today, and it asks two questions: do you publish bus maps of the individual towns in your area, and if not what would it take to commission one. It cites the Enhanced Partnership multi-operator information requirement, which is current, statutory and dated 2026.
- **The reader sends it. We never do, and there is no control on the page that would.** No recipient address, no mailto, no form, no queue. A relay would need consent machinery this project does not have; a council answers a resident of its own area and files a supplier's round robin; and by the tenth one a relay reads as spam from us. The test asserts the *absence* of a mailto and of a submit control, because that is the half a reader of the rendered page would never notice going missing.
- **"Put it in your own words" is the sentence that makes the first rule true.** Every reader gets a word-identical letter, so fifty residents sending fifty identical letters have sent a campaign — the same object the "you send it, not us" rule was trying not to be, wearing a different return address. The text cannot fix that and only the reader can, so the note above the letter says plainly that a short letter that sounds like them is worth ten identical ones, and that what follows is a starting point rather than a form. No check can hold that property; the check holds the sentence.
- **The letter was read back before it shipped, and three things in it were wrong.** It opened by saying no map existed and then, for every authority publishing a network map but no town maps — the largest group in the directory — said two lines later that it could see their network map. Its first question asked whether they publish a map of the place searched for, which for a county they generally do, so the exchange would have ended with both sides correct and nothing gained; it now asks about the individual towns in their area. And it said the network map "is useful for planning a journey across the area", an opinion about a map the sender may never have opened. Also cut: an unsourced claim that a map costs a small fraction of running the services, which is a supplier's argument in a resident's mouth.
- **"Dear Sir or Madam", with the authority on a `To:` line above it.** *Yours faithfully* pairs with *Sir or Madam*; a named addressee takes *sincerely*, and "Dear Essex County Council … Yours faithfully" is a mismatch an officer notices in a letter whose whole credibility rests on reading as a resident's own. The recipient line keeps the authority's name in the copied text, so a reader holding two letters can still tell them apart. It is the organisation's name and never a contact address.
- **The letter does not pitch us, and the paragraph that does says so.** A separate, clearly labelled block offers a sentence naming busmaps.uk as a supplier, and the reader adds it or does not — it is not in the letter and not in what the copy button copies. A resident's letter we ghost-wrote to recommend our own service, with nothing to say whose words those were, is astroturf whatever it says.
- **It is in the page, not behind JavaScript.** A `<details>` block with the letter in a `<pre>`, rendered server-side like the rest of the panel; the copy button is written `hidden` and unhidden only where there is a clipboard to write to, because a dead control is worse than none and the reader this exists for is the one who already got no map.
- **No letter where a map of that town already exists.** A hit on a town name means the directory records a map *of* that town, and the card has just linked it. `searchDirectory()` now carries out *how* a row matched so the card can tell the two apart.
- **And a count of who asked.** A submitted search that matches no map of ours adds one to a tally of place names — `search_demand`, one row per place, a count, and the first and last **date**. It is evidence for the CIC papers: how many people come looking for a map of somewhere and find nothing.
- **A TALLY, NOT A LOG, and the difference is the whole design.** The standing promise that search queries are never written to a log (P9 B8) is untouched — `logRedaction.js` and the Caddyfile both still drop the query string, and a test here asserts they do. The table has no address column, no session, no user agent and no timestamp: `first_seen` and `last_seen` are dates, so nothing in it can say when in the day a search happened or put two searches in order. Anything typed that is not a bare place name — an email, a postcode, a whole question — is counted with its text discarded. `public/legal.html` now says all of this, because a thing the privacy page does not mention is a thing we should not be doing.
- **Keystrokes are not searches.** The box fires on a 300ms debounce, so one reader typing "Beaconsfield" arrives a dozen times. Only a submitted search counts — the client sends `intent=submit` on Enter or the button and never while typing, and a `/maps?q=` arrival counts because reaching a URL is itself deliberate.
- **Falsified in both directions.** `prove-red-suggest.mjs` breaks each of the letter's five rules in turn, ten arms — the pitch folded into the body, a mailto added, the town rule dropped, the copy button unhidden, the "own words" note deleted, the authority greeted by name again — and requires the named check to go red. `prove-red-demand.mjs` does the same for the tally, and its arm 4 is the one that matters: it adds a `created_at` column, which is the change that would turn the tally into a log while every report still printed and every other test stayed green. Arm 3 breaks the shape filter the *other* way, so that a filter rejecting everything cannot pass a test that only looks at what it refuses.
- **One thing found on the way.** The existing `prove-red-directory.mjs` had an arm anchored on a line this change edited, and it correctly reported "the patch changed nothing" rather than passing — a falsification harness catching its own subject moving under it. It is re-anchored.
